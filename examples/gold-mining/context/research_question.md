# Research question

**How do gold mining operations directly and indirectly affect biodiversity?**

This corpus addresses the causal pathways from gold mining to biodiversity outcomes, distinguishing:

- **Direct impacts:** habitat removal (deforestation, soil excavation), water-channel modification, toxic releases (mercury, cyanide), sediment loading.
- **Indirect impacts:** miner influx via roads and prices, governance failure and informality, displacement of indigenous land rights, mercury bioaccumulation through aquatic food webs, downstream human health and wildlife exposure.

## Working hypotheses

1. **Direct biophysical pathway.** Gold mining causes deforestation, soil disturbance, mercury and cyanide pollution, and sediment loading; these directly reduce species abundance and ecosystem function.
2. **Indirect socio-economic pathway.** Higher gold prices and weak governance promote miner influx; miner influx is amplified by roads and proximity to settlements; protected areas, indigenous land rights, and enforcement reduce mining intensity but with limited reach.
3. **Biogeochemical-transport pathway.** Mercury released by amalgamation is transported through aquatic systems, bioaccumulates in fish, and is consumed by humans and piscivorous wildlife with delayed effects.

## Scope of this run

- **Geographic:** primary coverage Peruvian Amazon, Brazilian Amazon, Ecuadorian Andes, French Guiana, Indonesian Sulawesi, southeast Australian box-ironbark forests, and tropical reviews. Cited literature spans broader pan-tropical scope.
- **Temporal:** 1997–2022 publication window.
- **Mining type:** primarily artisanal and small-scale gold mining (ASGM); some coverage of mining-company concessions.
- **Language:** English.
